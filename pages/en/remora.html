The ideaA drone that tags along

A remora is a fish that attaches itself to a larger animal and travels wherever it goes. Remora the drone does the same with a person: it follows on its own, without anyone steering it.

The first prototype follows a walker who carries a small GPS beacon; following by camera alone comes later. It counts as working when it stays 5 to 15 m from a walking person through five two-minute runs, with turns, a U-turn and a stop, without the pilot touching the sticks.

Above, the core of the problem as a toy: the drone is pulled toward a point behind the person by a spring and slowed by a damper. Too little damping and it wobbles; too much and it lags.

The aircraftTake it apart

A one-piece frame printed in nylon, a deck of guard rings around the propellers, and over the electronics a shell shaped like the common remora: flat head, sucking disc with eighteen pairs of lamellae, projecting lower jaw, high pectoral fins and a large tail. The GPS sits under the disc, the part a real remora holds on with. The shell clips on without tools and stays clear of every propeller’s airflow. The parts drawn in colour are designed for Remora; the rest are bought parts, modelled at their published size.

The whole design lives in code. The frame is a parametric model in build123d, and a script iterates propulsion, geometry and mass until they agree: 197 g all-up, a thrust-to-weight ratio of 7 and 10–12 minutes per battery. Another script checks every part against every other for collisions and measures the clearances the design depends on. The printed parts are MJF nylon: lighter than the usual PETG and as strong in every direction, which matters in a crash.

StructureArms tuned against vibration

A printed arm is a spring with a motor on its end. If its natural frequency lands on the propeller’s rotation speed, it shakes, and the flight controller reads the shaking as motion. Each arm is modelled as a tapered cantilever, and a script picks the rib height so the first bending mode sits clear of both the rotor frequency in hover and the blade-pass frequency, with margin for the uncertain stiffness of the printed material.

Strength is not the limit: at full thrust the root of the arm sees about 1 MPa, more than forty times below what the nylon can take. Stiffness sets the size.

SoftwareThe follow controller

The walker wears a beacon: a GPS receiver and a small radio board with its own battery, sending its position five times a second. A second board on the drone receives it and runs Remora’s follow controller. When the pilot switches the autopilot into its guided mode, the controller remembers where the drone sits relative to the walker, smooths the incoming positions, and sends the autopilot a target for every fix: the walker’s position plus that offset, the walker’s velocity, and a heading that faces them. If the beacon goes quiet, the targets stop and the autopilot holds position.

The controller was tested against the real autopilot software. The exact code that runs on the board was compiled into a library and flown against ArduPilot’s own simulator, with a virtual walker carrying a beacon with drifting GPS error and lost packets, along the same route as the acceptance test.

The first run showed the drone’s speed jittering with the GPS noise. A filter that predicts with the GPS’s own velocity measurement cut that jitter by a third without adding lag. When the beacon was cut for six seconds, the drone stopped within 3.5 s and picked the walker up again afterwards.

AnalysisHow far from the walker

Both the walker and the drone know their position only through GPS, and each GPS drifts slowly by a metre or two. What moves the drone is the difference between the two errors. A Monte Carlo of 400 simulated walks per point shows which starting distance leaves the most room on both sides of the band.

Starting 10 m behind is best at every level of drift, which sets how the drone is placed before the follow is switched on.
