Why

Most savings calculators give one number: assume 7 % a year, here is your pension. A physicist wants the distribution and the error bar, and wants to see the code get the right answer on a case where the answer is known before trusting it on one where it isn’t.

The questions

QuestionMethod
What do returns look like?Normal vs Student-t fits, tail index, variance-ratio test
How bad can it get?VaR and expected shortfall, drawdown episodes, VaR backtests

Would a strategy have worked?Look-ahead-free backtests, bootstrap error bars, deflated Sharpe ratio
What will my savings plan reach?Monte Carlo with German fund tax and a withdrawal phase

The data

European ETFs only exist since about 2009, which misses both big crashes of the 2000s. So the history is extended to 1999 from official sources: the Fama-French developed-market return converted to euros, and a synthetic 10-year Bund priced from Bundesbank yields. Each month the bond is bought at par and revalued at the new yield:

R = c·a(y₁, M − Δ) + (1 + y₁)−(M−Δ) − 1 + cΔ   ≈ carry − duration × Δy

Both proxies are checked against real ETFs where the two overlap:

Checking it

Every model is tested against something it has to reproduce: a closed-form result, an exact identity or an established library. For a savings plan under geometric Brownian motion, the mean and variance of final wealth are known exactly, so the Monte Carlo error can be measured directly:

The drawdown check needed one correction: a simulation that only looks at discrete times misses part of every peak and trough. Adding the Broadie-Glasserman-Kou continuity term, about 2 × 0.58 σ√Δt, brings it within one standard error. The whole suite runs on every push.

What it found

  • A savings plan is a distribution, not a number. Saving 500 € a month for 30 years in a 60/40 portfolio, after costs and German tax, ends at a median of about 320 000 € in today’s money, with a 5–95 % range of 180 000 to 570 000 € (chart above, followed by 30 years of 4 % withdrawals).
  • Recent history flatters equities. Since 2010 the worst month-end drawdown of a world ETF was about 19 %. Since 1999 the same exposure lost 52 % and needed over 12 years to get back to its 2000 peak.
  • The normal distribution fails in the tail. A Gaussian 99 % VaR was breached in 3.7 % of months instead of 1 %.
  • Bonds stopped hedging. From 2000 to 2020 the stock-bond correlation was mostly negative, often around −0.3. After the 2022 inflation shock it turned clearly positive.
  • Good backtests can still be luck. Trend following had the best Sharpe ratio of nine strategies, but its bootstrap confidence interval still includes zero improvement.
  • The 4 % rule is optimistic for euro investors. The withdrawal rate that lasted 30 years in 95 % of scenarios was 2.7–3.2 %, depending on the allocation.

What went wrong

  • The first bond proxy only correlated 0.57 with the ETF. The yield series was a monthly average, which smears every move across two months. Switching to daily Bundesbank yields sampled at month end raised it to 0.95.
  • A statistic was off by a factor q. The variance ratio divided by the horizon twice. The test that caught it uses theory: for an AR(1) process, VR(2) = 1 + φ exactly.
  • A retiree’s savings were never invested. With no saving phase, the starting lump sum skipped the purchase step. Writing the test for “12 000 € at zero return lasts exactly 12 months of 1 000 €” exposed it.
  • The money-weighted return overflowed on long histories. (1 − 0.99)−300 is not a number a computer likes. The fix computes discount factors in log space.

Limits

Twenty-seven years of monthly data contain only a few independent bear markets, so long-horizon numbers carry wide uncertainty. The German tax model is a planning simplification. None of this is investment advice.
