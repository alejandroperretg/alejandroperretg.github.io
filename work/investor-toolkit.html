Why

Most savings calculators give one number: assume 7 % a year, here is your pension. A physicist wants the distribution and the error bar, and wants to see the code get the right answer on a case where the answer is known before trusting it on one where it isn’t.

In plain terms

TermMeaning
VolatilityTypical yearly swing. 12 % means that in about two years out of three, the return lands within 12 points of its average.
DrawdownFall from the previous high. 10 000 falling to 4 750 is a 52 % drawdown.
CorrelationWhether two assets move together: +1 always together, 0 unrelated, −1 always opposite.
SigmaThe typical size of a move. A 5-sigma day is five times larger than a typical day.
Monte CarloReplaying thousands of possible market histories, built from real months, to see the range of outcomes.
Today’s moneyAmounts corrected for inflation, so they buy what they would buy today.

One plan, 100 futures

Save 500 € a month for 30 years in a 60/40 portfolio: 180 000 € paid in. After costs and German tax, the middle outcome is about 320 000 € in today’s money. Each dot is one of 100 representative futures: the worst ends near 130 000 €, the best above 750 000 €, and 6 in 100 end below what was paid in (dark dots).

A real crash

10 000 € put into world stocks at the August 2000 peak was worth 4 751 € by March 2003. It took until February 2013 to get back to 10 000 €, and it had grown to about 50 600 € by 2026. Anyone who had to sell in 2003 lost half; anyone who could wait was rewarded.

Extreme days

How often did the world-stock ETF move by more than a few “typical days” since 2010, compared with a bell-curve model?

Seventeen years of data contain ten days the bell curve expects once in seven millennia. Risk models built on it underestimate crashes: a “1-in-100 months” loss limit from such a model was broken in 3.7 months out of 100.

Bonds as protection

In 2002 and 2008 German government bonds rose while stocks fell 30 % and 37 %. In 2022, when inflation pushed interest rates up, both fell together. Bonds protect against recessions, not against inflation.

Spreading risk

One German blue chip typically swings about 26 % a year. Five of them together still swing about 20 %, because they share one economy. The world index swings about 12 %: diversification helps most when the assets are truly different.

How much to withdraw

Retire with 500 000 €, withdraw a fixed amount raised with inflation each year, for 30 years. Each square is one of 100 simulated histories; dark squares ran out of money. The popular 4 % rule failed in 16 of 100; withdrawing 3 % failed in 3.

Skill or luck?

A simple trend-following rule turned 1 € into 8.0 € since 2002, against 6.8 € for buying and holding, with smaller crashes. Impressive, until history is reshuffled 2 000 times: in about 9 of 100 reshuffles it comes out behind. That is not strong enough to call it skill, which is the honest answer most backtests never give.

The data

European ETFs only exist since about 2009, which misses both big crashes of the 2000s. So the history is extended to 1999 from official sources: the Fama-French developed-market return converted to euros, and a synthetic 10-year Bund priced from Bundesbank yields. Each month the bond is bought at par and revalued at the new yield:

R = c·a(y₁, M − Δ) + (1 + y₁)−(M−Δ) − 1 + cΔ   ≈ carry − duration × Δy

Both proxies are checked against real ETFs where the two overlap:

Checking it

Before trusting the simulator on a question with no known answer, it has to get the right answer on questions that have one, the same way a structural solver is first tested on a beam with a textbook solution. For example, simulating four times more histories should halve the error, and it does.

Every model is tested against something it has to reproduce: a closed-form result, an exact identity or an established library. For a savings plan under geometric Brownian motion, the mean and variance of final wealth are known exactly, so the Monte Carlo error can be measured directly:

The drawdown check needed one correction: a simulation that only looks at discrete times misses part of every peak and trough. Adding the Broadie-Glasserman-Kou continuity term, about 2 × 0.58 σ√Δt, brings it within one standard error. The whole suite runs on every push.

What went wrong

  • The first bond proxy only correlated 0.57 with the ETF. The yield series was a monthly average, which smears every move across two months. Switching to daily Bundesbank yields sampled at month end raised it to 0.95.
  • A statistic was off by a factor q. The variance ratio divided by the horizon twice. The test that caught it uses theory: for an AR(1) process, VR(2) = 1 + φ exactly.
  • A retiree’s savings were never invested. With no saving phase, the starting lump sum skipped the purchase step. Writing the test for “12 000 € at zero return lasts exactly 12 months of 1 000 €” exposed it.
  • The money-weighted return overflowed on long histories. (1 − 0.99)−300 is not a number a computer likes. The fix computes discount factors in log space.

Limits

Twenty-seven years of monthly data contain only a few independent bear markets, so long-horizon numbers carry wide uncertainty. The German tax model is a planning simplification. None of this is investment advice.
