Investor’s Toolkit

Six questions a long-term investor asks, answered with euro-area market data since 1999 and with models checked the way I would check a simulation.

Why

Most savings calculators give one number: assume 7% a year, and here is your pension. A physicist wants the distribution and the error bar, and wants the code to reproduce a case with a known answer before trusting it with one that has none.

All amounts below are in today’s money, corrected for inflation, unless marked otherwise. Market data run to 2026.

In plain terms

TermMeaning
VolatilityThe typical yearly swing. At 12%, the return lands within 12 points of its average in about two years out of three.
DrawdownThe fall from the previous high. €10,000 falling to €4,750 is a 52% drawdown.
CorrelationWhether two assets move together: +1 always together, 0 unrelated, −1 always opposite.
SigmaThe typical size of a move. A 5-sigma day is five times larger than a typical day.
Monte CarloSimulating thousands of possible market histories, built from real months, to see the range of outcomes.

A plan is a range

Save €500 a month for 30 years in 60% world stocks and 40% German government bonds, raising the amount with inflation: €180,000 paid in. After fund costs and German tax, including the tax on the final sale, the middle outcome is €289,000, 1.6 times the money paid in. Each dot is one of 100 representative futures. The worst ends with €129,000, the best with €645,000, and 7 end below the amount paid in (dark dots). The chart at the top shows wealth before that final tax, because a retiree who withdraws gradually never sells everything at once.

Crashes take time

€10,000 put into world stocks at the August 2000 peak was worth €4,751 by March 2003. On the account statement it was back to €10,000 in February 2013; after inflation, only in August 2014. By 2026 it had grown to €50,600, or €28,800 in today’s money. Anyone forced to sell in 2003 lost half; anyone who could wait was rewarded.

Part of the excess comes from volatility clustering: calm and turbulent periods alternate, and one bell curve fitted to both misjudges each. Measured against the volatility of the preceding weeks, 4 of the 10 extreme days remain, still more than a thousand times what the bell curve allows. A risk model needs both effects.

Bonds and inflation

In 2002 and 2008, German government bonds gained while world stocks lost 30% and 37%. In 2022 both fell: rising inflation pushed interest rates up, and rising rates lower bond prices. In this sample, bonds cushioned recessions but not inflation. Three years illustrate the pattern; they do not prove a law.

Spreading risk

A single German blue-chip stock typically swings about 26% in a year. Five of them together still swing about 20%, because the companies share one economy. The world index swings about 12%. Diversification pays when the holdings are genuinely different.

How much to withdraw

Retire with €500,000 in the 60/40 portfolio and withdraw a fixed amount each year, raised with inflation. Each square is one of 100 simulated 30-year histories; dark squares ran out of money. Withdrawing 4% failed in 16 of 100; withdrawing 3% failed in 3.

Skill or luck?

A simple trend-following rule turned €1 into €8.0 since 2002, against €6.8 for buying and holding, and its worst fall was 20% instead of 48%. Resampling the history 2,000 times, it came out behind in about 9 cases out of 100, too often to rule out luck. The test also ignores taxes: the rule trades about three times the portfolio’s value each year, and in a German taxable account every sale realises gains.

The data

European ETFs exist only from about 2009, which misses both crashes of the 2000s. The history is therefore rebuilt back to 1999 from official sources: the Fama-French developed-market return converted to euros, and a synthetic 10-year Bund priced from Bundesbank yields. Each month the bond is bought at par and revalued at the new yield:

R = c·a(y₁, M − Δ) + (1 + y₁)−(M−Δ) − 1 + cΔ   ≈ carry − duration × Δy

Both proxies are checked against real ETFs over the years in which both exist:

Checking it

A structural solver is trusted only after it reproduces the textbook deflection of a simple beam. The same rule applies here. For a savings plan under geometric Brownian motion, the mean and variance of final wealth are known exactly, so the simulation error can be measured directly: four times more simulated histories should halve it, and they do.

The drawdown check needed one correction: a simulation that only looks at discrete times misses part of every peak and trough. Adding the Broadie-Glasserman-Kou continuity term, about 2 × 0.58 σ√Δt, brings it within one standard error. The whole suite runs on every push.

What went wrong

  • The first bond proxy correlated only 0.57 with the ETF. The yield series was a monthly average, which smears each move across two months. Daily Bundesbank yields sampled at month end raised the correlation to 0.95.
  • A statistic was off by a factor q. The variance ratio divided by the horizon twice. A test based on theory caught it: for an AR(1) process, VR(2) = 1 + φ exactly.
  • A retiree’s savings were never invested. Without a saving phase, the starting lump sum skipped the purchase step. The test “€12,000 at zero return lasts exactly 12 months of €1,000” exposed it.
  • The money-weighted return overflowed on long histories. Discounting at −99% a month over 300 months means computing 100300 = 10600, far beyond the largest 64-bit float. The fix works in logarithms.
  • The advance tax was booked a year early. German law treats each year’s Vorabpauschale as received in the following January, so it uses that year’s tax allowance. A review caught it; the tests now check the timing.

Limits

Twenty-seven years of data contain few bear markets, so long-horizon numbers are uncertain. The tax model is a planning simplification, the backtests are pre-tax, and the risk models do not forecast volatility. None of this is investment advice.
